The useful question after signing an enterprise AI contract is: what can an employee actually do, and what evidence will show that the workflow worked?

For the company AI rollout I am documenting, internal approval and contract signing are complete. Employee access rollout is still in progress. An education and operations plan was also part of the planned next stage.

That is the scope of this company snapshot. It does not establish completed training, accepted employee work, or productivity gains. The operating checks below are a proposed playbook for the next stage, rather than a report that these checks have already been completed.

## Make the AI rollout status specific

“AI adoption completed” is too broad for this stage. It can hide several different questions: whether the organization has authorized AI use, whether employees can access the intended workspace, whether an AI output is useful, and whether the workflow is used again.

For this rollout, the status I can report is narrower:

| AI rollout milestone | Current evidence in this note |
|---|---|
| Internal approval for the enterprise AI rollout | Completed, based on my rollout update |
| Enterprise AI contract signing | Completed, based on my rollout update |
| Employee access rollout | In progress; full activation is not established |
| AI education and operations plan | Planned; current completion is not established |
| Accepted employee work, repeat use, and productivity effects | Not established by the evidence presented here |

A status like “in progress” should lead to a specific next check. For AI access, that means verifying what an intended employee can do in the intended workspace, rather than treating an invitation as a successful start.

## Five checks to move from an AI contract to an operating workflow

This is a proposed sequence for a bounded AI workflow. Different tasks may need additional conditions or a different sequence.

| AI workflow gate | Evidence to collect | Next action when evidence is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Authorization | An owner identifies the task, permitted inputs, output recipient, and approval boundary | Clarify the task and input boundary before using company material |
| Employee access | A representative employee signs in and completes an approved starting task | Record the access problem and verify the corrected path |
| Task acceptance | A reviewer checks an AI output against the original input and agreed acceptance criteria | Preserve the rejected output and identify the correction needed |
| Supported use | An employee can repeat the task and knows how to report uncertainty or failure | Improve the worked example, instructions, or support route |
| Repeat use and evaluation | The accepted workflow is used again, with records of review effort and relevant outcomes | Investigate the reason for non-use before claiming adoption or expanding automation |

These checks distinguish permission, access, output quality, and actual use. They also give the rollout owner a concrete next action when a stage remains unresolved.

## Teach an AI task with a reviewable result

My proposed first exercise is a source-checked AI summary: give the learner an approved, non-sensitive source pack and ask for a short summary that links each material claim to its source.

The learner should also identify missing information. A reviewer can then compare the summary with the source pack, check for unsupported claims, and decide whether the result is usable.

The same task can later become part of an AI automation workflow. Before implementing or scheduling that workflow, map its permitted inputs, processing steps, review point, failure path, and destination. If an input or permission is missing, the workflow should record the reason and stop at that boundary.

Training then has something concrete to teach: an input, an AI-assisted action, an acceptance decision, and a way to recover when the result is inadequate.

## Keep a small AI workflow evidence card

For a first pilot, I would start with this reusable record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| AI task and owner | The intended work and person responsible for its operation |
| Input and method version | The approved input and prompt or automation version |
| Output and review | The result, review decision, and corrections |
| Actual use | Whether the accepted output entered the intended work |
| Repeat use | Whether the workflow was used again, and over what period |
| Baseline and observation period | The comparison process, unit of work, and observation window |
| Effort and exceptions | Preparation, AI processing, review, rework, and failed attempts |
| Next change | What to retain, revise, or test next |

This is a proposed record, not a measurement result. It makes a useful distinction: producing a file, accepting it, and using it are separate observations.

OpenAI Academy similarly distinguishes participation or interest from AI use in real work and repeated use. Its guidance also separates efficiency signals from quality and operating evidence. That supports the distinction; it does not prove anything about our company rollout. [Gather appropriate evidence of value](https://academy.openai.com/public/clubs/champions-ecqup/resources/gather-appropriate-evidence-of-value-2026-07-17).

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework emphasizes testing in deployment context, feedback, and ongoing monitoring. My proposed sequence is not a NIST checklist or a claim of conformance. [AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/).

The next company milestone worth reporting is a bounded AI task that an employee can access, a reviewer can accept, and the team can use again. Until we have that evidence, the accurate update remains: approval and contract signing completed; employee access rollout in progress.
